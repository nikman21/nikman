# Design Research

Date: 2026-08-01

## Research goal

Find references that help Nik's site feel like a public identity and working journal: precise enough to reflect a systems builder, warm enough to hold faith and humanity, and strange enough to suggest an original future. References were selected for reusable principles, not surface novelty.

## References

### 1. Craig Mod

Source: https://craigmod.com/

What works: exceptional long-form typography, quiet margins, direct personal voice, and an archive that feels accumulated rather than manufactured.

What does not fit: the site is more writer-first and bookish than Nik's builder identity requires.

Borrow: patient reading widths, strong essay hierarchy, and treating the personal site as a long-lived home.

### 2. Andy Matuschak

Source: https://andymatuschak.org/

What works: research is presented as active inquiry; projects, notes, and letters have distinct roles without feeling like separate brands.

What does not fit: the density and research-tool framing can become too academic.

Borrow: questions as navigation, visible work in progress, and explicit relationships between projects and ideas.

### 3. Andy Matuschak's working notes

Source: https://notes.andymatuschak.org/

What works: spatial reading, linked thought, compact metadata, and a system that reveals how ideas develop.

What does not fit: stacked-note interaction is too specialized for the main public site.

Borrow: small maturity/context labels and meaningful cross-links inside writing.

### 4. Maggie Appleton's Garden

Source: https://maggieappleton.com/garden

What works: structured content types, honest growth stages, illustrated thinking, and a garden that feels tended over years.

What does not fit: the playful illustration language is more whimsical than this direction.

Borrow: content collections, maturity metadata, and a clear distinction between essays, notes, patterns, and small observations.

### 5. Laurel Schwulst

Source: https://laurelschwulst.com/

What works: personal authorship, warm off-white ground, unusual but simple navigation, and the sense that a website can be a living world.

What does not fit: the expressive taxonomy can feel intentionally idiosyncratic and less immediately legible.

Borrow: lightness, confidence, and a homepage that exposes the connectedness of work without turning into a conventional portfolio.

### 6. Steven Holl Architects

Source: https://www.stevenholl.com/

What works: architecture, philosophy, research, watercolor, and news coexist in a rigorous editorial frame. The work is tied to light, landscape, and place.

What does not fit: a very large project archive and institutional scale would overstate Nik's current stage.

Borrow: concept-led project pages, restrained grids, and treating imagery as evidence of a point of view.

### 7. Norm Architects

Source: https://normcph.com/

What works: calm pacing, tactile materiality, large image moments, and minimalism that remains warm.

What does not fit: luxury styling and muted photography could make the site too polished or lifestyle-oriented.

Borrow: generous spacing, few high-value images, and nature framed rather than used as a full-site theme.

### 8. MIT Media Lab

Source: https://www.media.mit.edu/

What works: a research institution communicates imagination, experimentation, and future-facing work through modular editorial content.

What does not fit: the institutional breadth and high content volume would overwhelm a personal site.

Borrow: future optimism, cross-disciplinary framing, and concise project/research labeling.

### 9. MIT Design Lab

Source: https://design-lab.mit.edu/

What works: typographic scale, Swiss influence, and acknowledgment of the systems behind the site.

What does not fit: the visual tone can become formal and academic.

Borrow: disciplined typographic ratios and the idea that the site itself can document its construction.

### 10. IBM Design Language — Technical Diagrams

Source: https://www.ibm.com/design/language/infographics/technical-diagrams/design/

What works: diagrams use consistent grids, orthogonal connectors, precise labels, accessible line styles, and restrained node types.

What does not fit: enterprise diagram components would make the site feel like documentation.

Borrow: eight-pixel discipline, tiny left-aligned labels, clear legends when meaning is encoded, and diagrams only when they explain a real relationship.

### 11. Teenage Engineering

Source: https://teenage.engineering/

What works: industrial typography, product-manual labeling, bold limited colors, and a playful but engineered object language.

What does not fit: too much product-interface mimicry would make the site gadget-like.

Borrow: compact technical labels, hard color separation, square geometry, and confidence in a few unusual details.

### 12. Emergence Magazine

Source: https://emergencemagazine.org/

What works: nature, spirituality, ecology, and long-form writing are given cinematic scale without becoming travel content.

What does not fit: immersive editorial art direction on every story would be expensive and could overpower Nik's work.

Borrow: use landscape to hold wonder, attention, and humanity; reserve it for consequential moments.

### 13. The Long Now Foundation

Source: https://longnow.org/

What works: time is made tangible through language, machinery, archives, and a visually distinct long-horizon identity.

What does not fit: monumentality and civilizational scale could feel performative on a young founder's site.

Borrow: date and scale notation, questions about what endures, and a visible relationship between present work and a longer horizon.

### 14. Pioneer Works Broadcast

Source: https://pioneerworks.org/broadcast

What works: art, science, literature, and technology share one editorial voice; typography and issue structure give disparate subjects coherence.

What does not fit: magazine density and frequent art direction would distract from a personal identity.

Borrow: issue-like labels, clear content categories, and strong editorial rows rather than card grids.

### 15. The Creative Independent

Source: https://thecreativeindependent.com/

What works: a warm, practical editorial resource with memorable identity and clear reading paths.

What does not fit: its community-resource framing is broader and more instructional than Nik's journal.

Borrow: approachable seriousness, simple pathways, and design that supports creative honesty.

### 16. Works in Progress

Source: https://worksinprogress.co/

What works: optimistic writing about technology and progress, substantial article presentation, and confident typographic hierarchy.

What does not fit: the magazine voice is institutional rather than personal.

Borrow: ambitious subjects presented without startup spectacle and an archive that privileges strong ideas.

### 17. Stripe Press

Source: https://press.stripe.com/

What works: technical and historical subjects feel literary; book presentation uses strong typography and selective color.

What does not fit: commercial publishing polish can feel too finished for a work-in-progress identity.

Borrow: pairing engineering subjects with editorial dignity and letting each work have a precise visual identifier.

### 18. NASA Brand Center

Source: https://www.nasa.gov/nasa-brand-center/

What works: optimistic modernism, durable typography, high-contrast labeling, and visual systems built for technical clarity.

What does not fit: literal space imagery, mission patches, and agency branding would turn influence into costume.

Borrow: confidence, sparse technical captions, and 1970s–1980s future optimism.

### 19. JPL — Visions of the Future

Source: https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/galleries/visions-of-the-future/

What works: old travel-poster language imagines future places through reduced palettes, hard shapes, and bold composition.

What does not fit: planets, rockets, and destination-poster copy would make the site science-fiction themed.

Borrow: limited-color image processing and the feeling of a future imagined with analog production tools.

### 20. SPACE10 archive

Source: https://space10.com/

What works: experimental projects are treated as documented research rather than startup feature launches.

What does not fit: studio-scale research framing could overstate the maturity of small experiments.

Borrow: honest prototypes, field-study labeling, and an archive that can hold speculative work without pretending every idea is a product.

## Repeated patterns

Across the strongest references:

- One organizing idea creates more identity than many decorative motifs.
- Typography and spacing carry most of the interface.
- Image moments become memorable because they are rare and large.
- Personal sites feel credible when unfinished work is labeled honestly.
- Thin rules, indexes, dates, captions, and issue numbers create structure without cards.
- Nature works best when connected to attention, scale, time, or place.
- Technical diagrams succeed when they explain relationships, not when they decorate empty space.
- Strong archives separate finished essays, working notes, projects, and current activity.
- The best future-facing work is optimistic without using generic startup futurism.

## Direction extracted from research

The site should combine:

- Craig Mod's patience and reading quality.
- Maggie Appleton's structured, evolving content.
- Steven Holl's relationship between ideas, place, and image.
- IBM's disciplined diagram grammar.
- Teenage Engineering's engineered labels and hard color logic.
- Emergence's spiritual and natural scale.
- Long Now's temporal perspective.
- JPL's old-future color reduction.

The result is not a collage. It is a modern field journal whose interface is calm and exact while its landscape images appear to have passed through an old scientific imaging system.

## What to avoid

- Full-screen photography that reads as travel or startup branding.
- Card collections that flatten every item to equal importance.
- Decorative diagrams without a real concept.
- Nostalgic props, pixel-art interfaces, and retro-game motifs.
- Japanese clichés, space theming, or a fake research institution.
- Copy that implies a finished empire rather than an ambitious beginning.
